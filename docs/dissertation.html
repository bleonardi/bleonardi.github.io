Research Agenda

Institutional Misalignment and the Political Economy of American Urban Geography

American metropolitan institutions — governance structures, investment patterns, and knowledge production — are systematically miscalibrated to the actual spatial distribution of urban economic density. My dissertation documents three dimensions of this misalignment and their welfare consequences.

The measurement problem comes first: official political boundaries obscure where density actually lives, providing a distorted basis for every institutional decision downstream. Papers Two and Three then ask what happens when governance and fiscal allocation inherit that distortion. A fourth strand — still in early development — asks whether even knowledge production institutions reproduce the same miscalibration.

Paper One · The Sprawl Paradox

Redefining American Urbanity Through Density
Working paper →

Conventional US city population rankings are artifacts of 20th-century boundary consolidations, not measures of urban intensity. Indianapolis, Columbus, and Louisville absorbed their suburbs wholesale; Boston and San Francisco could not. The result is a ranking system where a 900,000-person metro that is 85% low-density exurb ranks above a 400,000-person metro that is almost entirely dense urban fabric.

This paper introduces the True Urban Core (TUC): the contiguous population residing in Census tracts exceeding 10,000 persons per square mile, a threshold consistent with the literature on transit viability, pedestrian activity, and agglomeration returns. When applied uniformly, the TUC reranking is dramatic. Phoenix, Indianapolis, and Louisville collapse; Boston, San Francisco, and Washington D.C. rise to the top tier. The analysis also decomposes the “consolidation premium” — the share of a city’s official population attributable to absorbed low-density territory — for all 100 largest MSAs.

Contribution: Provides the measurement foundation for Papers Two and Three. Both downstream papers inherit the boundary-distortion problem; TUC offers a corrected spatial baseline.

Paper Two · The Fiscal and Governance Costs of Fragmentation

Transit Governance Friction and the Cross-Subsidy of Sprawl
Transit Fragmentation → · Transportation ROI →

This paper has two empirical exercises united by a single argument: when metropolitan governance and fiscal allocation are organized around political rather than economic boundaries, dense areas pay and dispersed areas benefit.

Exercise A — Transit Fragmentation Index (TFI): Using 2023 National Transit Database data across 480+ urbanized areas, I construct the TFI as the inverse of a ridership-weighted Herfindahl-Hirschman Index across transit agencies. Highly fragmented regions (e.g., San Francisco Bay Area, TFI: 17.0) exhibit significantly lower per-capita ridership than similarly-sized consolidated systems (e.g., New York, TFI: 6.7), controlling for density, income, and land area. The mechanism is Coasian: fragmentation introduces transaction costs at agency interfaces — incompatible fares, misaligned schedules, duplicated overhead — that act as a per-trip tax on riders in polycentric or cross-boundary commuting patterns.

Exercise B — Fiscal ROI of Transportation Investment: Within the 8-county OKI region, urban tracts generate approximately 17× more property tax revenue per acre than low-density tracts, yet receive the lowest per-acre federal transportation investment. I construct tract-level fiscal productivity measures (estimated property tax yield per acre) and match them to USASpending.gov federal award data geocoded to census geographies. The cross-density comparison reveals a structural cross-subsidy: the fiscal surplus generated by high-density urban land partially offsets the infrastructure maintenance liabilities of low-density periphery.

Contribution: Connects institutional fragmentation theory to observable welfare costs in two domains — mobility and fiscal geography — and documents that both pathologies are larger in metros with greater true-urban/official-boundary divergence (from Paper One).

Paper Three · Research Without Place

Do Federal Reserve Banks Study What Their Districts Produce?
Working paper →

If spatial misalignment affects governance and fiscal institutions, does it also affect epistemic institutions with explicit regional mandates? The Federal Reserve System offers a clean test: twelve Banks, each with a defined geographic district and a research department whose formal charge includes monitoring local economic conditions.

Using the OpenAlex API, I assemble a corpus of approximately 18,300 Fed working papers published between 2010 and 2024. I apply a keyword-assisted topic model (keyATM) with 10 seeded topics — Agriculture, Energy, Finance, Housing, Labor, Manufacturing, Monetary Policy, Technology, Trade, Inequality — and compute each Bank’s research portfolio as a probability vector over topics. I then construct each Bank’s district economic profile from BLS QCEW 2022 county-level employment data, aggregated using official Fed district–county crosswalks.

The alignment score — the Pearson correlation between a Bank’s research vector and its district employment vector — reveals striking heterogeneity. Midwest Banks (St. Louis, Kansas City, Minneapolis) are the most aligned; Boston, Philadelphia, and San Francisco are the most misaligned, with research portfolios that diverge substantially from local specialization. Energy is near zero despite Dallas’s dominant position in oil and gas employment; the explanation appears to be that San Francisco’s “energy” topic mass captures climate and grid research rather than fossil fuels.

Contribution: Extends the spatial misalignment thesis from service delivery institutions to knowledge production. Suggests that institutional geography is sticky not just in infrastructure and governance but in the questions economists choose to ask.

Connecting Thread

Each paper documents a different institutional response to the same underlying condition: American metropolitan space is organized around political jurisdictions that were drawn for historical, legal, and political reasons with little regard for the functional economic geography they overlay. The TUC establishes the extent of that divergence. The fragmentation and ROI papers show its costs in mobility and public finance. The Fed paper asks whether the researchers charged with understanding regional economies have themselves been captured by the same boundary logic.

Taken together, the three papers argue that the welfare costs of spatial misclassification are not limited to any single domain — they are a systemic feature of how American metropolitan institutions are designed and maintained.
