Research

My primary research interest is the spatial mismatch between American metropolitan institutions and the actual distribution of urban economic density. The projects below document this mismatch across three domains and trace its consequences for measurement, governance, and public finance.

The measurement problem: Official city population figures are shaped by 20th-century boundary consolidations that have little to do with where people actually live at urban densities. The Sprawl Paradox introduces a corrected measure.

The governance and fiscal costs: When metropolitan governance and spending formulas inherit boundary distortions, dense areas systematically subsidize dispersed ones. The Transit Fragmentation and Transportation ROI analyses document this in mobility and public finance.

The epistemic question: Do the research institutions charged with understanding regional economies also reproduce the same boundary logic? The Federal Reserve alignment analysis is an early pass at this question.

A fourth strand — the Catholic parishes work — sits slightly outside this core thread but shares the same underlying concern: how legacy institutional boundaries misallocate resources in spatially heterogeneous environments.

Research Projects

The Sprawl Paradox

Redefining American Urbanity Through Density

US city population rankings are distorted by arbitrary political consolidations — Indianapolis, Columbus, and Louisville absorbed their suburbs wholesale; Boston and San Francisco could not. The result rewards administrative reach over actual density. This project introduces the True Urban Core (TUC): contiguous population in Census tracts exceeding 10,000 persons per square mile, the threshold associated with transit viability and walkable commercial corridors. When applied consistently, cities like Phoenix and Indianapolis drop sharply while Boston, SF, and DC emerge as the nation’s true urban heavyweights. A companion analysis extends the framework to the OKI region’s fiscal geography, showing that urban tracts generate roughly 17× more property tax revenue per acre than sprawl tracts while receiving less federal transportation investment per acre.

Transit Fragmentation

Quantifying Governance Friction in US Metropolitan Transit

In many US metro areas, transit service is divided among multiple independent authorities following legacy municipal boundaries rather than modern commuting patterns. This project introduces the Transit Fragmentation Index (TFI) — the inverse of a ridership-weighted HHI across agencies within each urbanized area — to quantify this institutional friction. Using 2023 National Transit Database data across 480+ urbanized areas, the analysis finds that fragmented regions suffer a persistent ridership penalty relative to consolidated systems of comparable size, even controlling for density, income, and built environment. In regions like Northern Kentucky and suburban Detroit, fragmentation accounts for a 55–79% loss in transit market share compared to demographically matched neighborhoods served by unified agencies.

Brent Spence Bridge: Home Price Event Study

An event study tracking residential home price responses to major Brent Spence Bridge corridor milestones — federal grant announcement, contractor award, groundbreaking. Identifies a significant “landing zone” effect: ZIP codes within 1 mile of the I-75/BSB corridor outperformed the broader Cincinnati–Northern Kentucky market by roughly 8.5 percentage points since the 2020 fire.

Canonical Rigidity: US Catholic Parishes

Spatial Mismatch and Institutional Boundaries in the US Catholic Church

A spatial econometric study of how 19th-century parish boundary structures misallocate sacramental services across modern urban demographics. Uses Voronoi tessellations to construct “natural market area” counterfactuals against canonical parish boundaries, quantifying spatial leakage where parishioners bypass assigned parishes for closer alternatives. A companion analysis estimates the opportunity cost of underutilized surface parking in high-density cores using Overture Maps building footprints.

Federal Reserve Research Alignment

Do Fed Banks Study What Their Districts Produce?

Topic-models ~18,300 Federal Reserve working papers (2010–2024) using keyATM with 10 seeded topics, then correlates each Bank’s research portfolio against district industry composition from BLS QCEW data. Midwest Feds show the strongest alignment; Boston, Philadelphia, and San Francisco are most misaligned, with research portfolios diverging substantially from local economic specialization. The Dallas Fed’s near-zero energy research score — despite oil and gas dominating district employment — appears to reflect that San Francisco captures climate and grid research under the same topic label.
