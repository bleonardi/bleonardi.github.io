Research

My research is fairly disparate in content but attempts to utilize the techniques favored by quantitative social science, especially economics, to answer questions that the technocractic approach tends to elide (or… just disregard as worth answering in the first place).

For example, much of my work tends to center on the friction between supposedly rationalistic, ‘science-driven’ institutions and structures shape how/what we see, measure. For instance, some work touches on:

Spatial and institutional mismatch is the most developed strand. Official city population figures, metropolitan governance structures, and federal spending formulas all inherit 20th-century boundary logics that diverge sharply from where urban density actually sits today. The Sprawl Paradox introduces a corrected density measure; Transit Fragmentation and Transportation ROI trace the fiscal and mobility costs; Federal Reserve alignment asks whether research institutions reproduce the same blind spots.

Institutional persistence connects the spatial work to a broader question about why organizations hold inherited structures even when those structures misallocate resources. The Catholic parishes analysis applies this lens to canonical boundary law; the papal continuity project extends it to the documentary record of magisterial teaching — testing whether Vatican II represents the radical rupture its critics and champions both claim.

Language as evidence runs through several projects. If institutions encode their commitments in text, then shifts in vocabulary, readability, and rhetorical structure are not merely stylistic — they are data. The street names project reads urban morphology through suffix drift; the papal project uses ITS and diff-in-diff on a 150-year corpus. The earlier HathiTrust NLP work is the methodological precursor to both.

The essays and philosophical pieces collected below make the normative commitments more explicit — they are the part of the work I cannot, and do not want to, hide behind the standard errors.

Research Projects

Mimetic Contagion and Collective Violence

Spatial evidence from lynching in the US Cotton Belt, 1884–1936

René Girard’s theory of mimetic contagion holds that collective violence spreads through social imitation — communities copy neighboring communities’ scapegoating, not just their grievances. This project provides the first systematic quantitative test of that claim, using a county-year panel of 2,614 Black lynching victims in the Cotton Belt (Tolnay-Beck + Seguin-Rigby combined). A spatial lag model with two-way fixed effects finds ρ = +0.054 (p < 0.001) under geographic adjacency — a near-doubling of lynching probability when neighboring counties lynched in the prior year. The core contribution is methodological: replacing the geographic weight matrix with three alternatives (railroad network, newspaper co-circulation, denominational similarity) to ask which transmission channel actually carried the contagion. Newspaper co-coverage produces the largest ρ (+0.101, p < 0.001), 88% above the geographic estimate; railroad (ρ=+0.041, p=0.041) and denomination (ρ=+0.041, p=0.019) channels also show significant effects. Mimesis traveled most powerfully through information networks — validating and extending Testa & Williams (QJE 2026) from elite signaling to horizontal crowd-to-crowd imitation.

The Sprawl Paradox

Redefining American Urbanity Through Density

US city population rankings are distorted by arbitrary political consolidations — Indianapolis, Columbus, and Louisville absorbed their suburbs wholesale; Boston and San Francisco could not. The result rewards administrative reach over actual density. This project introduces the True Urban Core (TUC): contiguous population in Census tracts exceeding 10,000 persons per square mile, the threshold associated with transit viability and walkable commercial corridors. When applied consistently, cities like Phoenix and Indianapolis drop sharply while Boston, SF, and DC emerge as the nation’s true urban heavyweights. A companion analysis extends the framework to the OKI region’s fiscal geography, showing that urban tracts generate roughly 17× more property tax revenue per acre than sprawl tracts while receiving less federal transportation investment per acre.

Transit Fragmentation

Quantifying Governance Friction in US Metropolitan Transit

In many US metro areas, transit service is divided among multiple independent authorities following legacy municipal boundaries rather than modern commuting patterns. This project introduces the Transit Fragmentation Index (TFI) — the inverse of a ridership-weighted HHI across agencies within each urbanized area — to quantify this institutional friction. Using 2023 National Transit Database data across 480+ urbanized areas, the analysis finds that fragmented regions suffer a persistent ridership penalty relative to consolidated systems of comparable size, even controlling for density, income, and built environment. In regions like Northern Kentucky and suburban Detroit, fragmentation accounts for a 55–79% loss in transit market share compared to demographically matched neighborhoods served by unified agencies.

Canonical Rigidity: US Catholic Parishes

Spatial Mismatch and Institutional Boundaries in the US Catholic Church

A spatial econometric study of how 19th-century parish boundary structures misallocate sacramental services across modern urban demographics. Uses Voronoi tessellations to construct “natural market area” counterfactuals against canonical parish boundaries, quantifying spatial leakage where parishioners bypass assigned parishes for closer alternatives. A companion analysis estimates the opportunity cost of underutilized surface parking in high-density cores using Overture Maps building footprints.

Continuity and Rupture in Papal Teaching

Did Vatican II Break with Tradition? A Text-as-Data Test

The “rupture narrative” holds that the Second Vatican Council (1962–65) marked a decisive break in Catholic teaching — celebrated by progressive Catholics, lamented by traditionalists, and largely taken for granted by both. This project tests that claim empirically against 150 years of papal encyclicals. Using interrupted time series (ITS), difference-in-differences, and within-tradition cosine similarity, the analysis asks whether Vatican II produced a detectable discontinuity in the style, complexity, vocabulary, and thematic emphasis of magisterial documents — or whether the textual record supports the hermeneutics of continuity advanced by Benedict XVI.

The analysis spans six traditions (Catholic, LDS, Anglican, SBC, Orthodox, and USCCB) and a corpus of nearly 2,000 documents. Across most measures, the Catholic encyclical series shows less discontinuity at 1965 than peer Christian traditions show at their own watershed moments. USCCB pastoral letters — which post-date the Council and thus cannot serve as a pre/post control — track Roman encyclicals closely in complexity and style, suggesting downstream coherence rather than rupture.

The project is methodologically careful about identification: regression discontinuity is rejected as a primary approach because popes demonstrably time documents strategically, violating the no-manipulation assumption (Paul VI held Humanae Vitae through the Council itself). ITS with robust standard errors is the preferred estimator.

What’s in a Street Name?

How street suffix types lost their meaning across eras of American development

Street suffixes — Road, Court, Boulevard, Lane — once carried typological meaning. This analysis tests empirically whether that meaning persists, using OpenStreetMap geometry across four states (Ohio, Kentucky, Rhode Island, Connecticut) cross-referenced with Census building-era data. The core finding: by the 1980s, most suffixes are statistically indistinguishable on intersection density and segment length. A divergence score benchmarks each suffix against its pre-modern archetype. Road remains the most honest suffix; Boulevard has drifted the farthest from its original form.

A spatial mimesis extension asks whether cul-de-sac adoption spread by neighborhood copying. Year-by-year OLS across 207,000 block groups finds mimetic spillover β rising from 0.60 in 1940 to a peak of 0.90 in the early 1980s — the height of the planning monoculture — then falling to 0.67 by 2010 as New Urbanism eroded the dominant design vocabulary.

Federal Reserve Research Alignment

Do Fed Banks Study What Their Districts Produce?

Topic-models ~18,300 Federal Reserve working papers (2010–2024) using keyATM with 10 seeded topics, then correlates each Bank’s research portfolio against district industry composition from BLS QCEW data. Midwest Feds show the strongest alignment; Boston, Philadelphia, and San Francisco are most misaligned, with research portfolios diverging substantially from local economic specialization. The Dallas Fed’s near-zero energy research score — despite oil and gas dominating district employment — appears to reflect that San Francisco captures climate and grid research under the same topic label.
